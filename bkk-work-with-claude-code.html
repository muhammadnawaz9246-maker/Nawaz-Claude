Part 1 · The setup

Claude as a coworker
with a desk

A folder on your own machine that Claude reads before it starts, and files its work back into.

The core shift

Chat forgets you.Claude Code remembers.

A normal chat starts from zero every time. You re-explain who you are, what tone you want, and what you're working on. Every single session.

Claude Code works inside a folder on your computer and reads your context files before it starts working. Your role, your writing rules, and your goals load automatically. Then it builds real files and saves them back to that folder.

Same capability, different context. A chat is a brilliant new hire on day one; you brief them from scratch every time. Claude Code is someone just as sharp who already knows the company inside out.

the mental model

It's a coworker with a desk

The desk is a folder on your machine. You keep your context on that desk. Claude reads it, does the job, and files the output. Nothing gets lost between sessions.

The whole trick

One folder, three rooms

Create a folder called Claude. Three subfolders and one note inside. That's the system.

  • ABOUT ME is the only folder Claude reads on its own, every task.
  • OUTPUTS is where finished work gets saved, one subfolder per project.
  • TEMPLATES holds the skeletons of your best work for reuse.
📁 Claude/ CLAUDE.md # the note, read every session 📁 ABOUT ME/ # read every task about-me.md anti-ai-writing-style.md my-department.md 📁 OUTPUTS/ # saved deliverables project-name/ 📁 TEMPLATES/ # reusable skeletons
ABOUT ME

The three files that do the heavy lifting

Keep all three under 6,000 tokens combined. Small files get read carefully. Big files get skimmed.

about-me.md

Who you are

Your role, how you work, your standards, what you hate, your rules. Under 2,000 words.

anti-ai-writing-style.md

How you write

The words you ban and the patterns that make you cringe. For Urdu and Punjabi work: register, script, and which terms stay untranslated.

my-department.md

What your team is working toward

Goals, the season calendar, who reads your work, what you're saying no to. Under 1,000 words.

Claude reads these before every task. You stop re-explaining yourself forever.

File 1 · about-me.md

Let Claude interview you

Don't write it from a blank page. Open Claude Code in your folder, on Opus, paste this, and answer out loud.

paste into a new Claude Code session 
You are building my about-me.md file for my Claude folder. This file will be read by Claude at the start of every session to help you do my job with me. It needs to be concise and high-signal.

Your job: interview me using AskUserQuestion (20 questions), then compile the answers into a condensed about-me.md under 2,000 tokens.

Interview rules: one question at a time. Let me use "Other" to dictate long answers. If I'm vague, push back and ask for a specific example. Follow interesting threads before moving on.

Cover: who I am (role, department at Bakhabar Kissan, who I work with, a good week), how I work (daily tools, how I start a task, how I review, what "done" means), what good looks like, what I hate, my hard rules, the languages I work in and when each one is used, and my contrarian opinions.

Output: a single markdown file. Do NOT save raw Q&A. Extract patterns into condensed prose and bullets. End with a section "Instructions for Claude" holding 10 numbered do/don't rules. Under 2,000 tokens. Save as about-me.md in my ABOUT ME/ folder.

Talk your answers. You give more context speaking than typing, and it sounds like you.

File 2 · anti-ai-writing-style.md

The file that kills the robot voice

Without this, Claude writes like Claude. With it, Claude writes like you, minus the parts you hate.

List the words you ban. Kill the reframe pattern. Set your formatting rules. Content and Field teams: add your language rules here too. A starter is on the right, build from it.

The reframe pattern, "this isn't X, this is Y," is the single most reliable AI tell in English. Ban it first. Then add the Urdu equivalents you keep seeing.

anti-ai-writing-style.md · starter 
# MY WRITING RULES

## Voice
- Short paragraphs. 1-2 sentences, 3 max.
- Vary sentence length. No metronome rhythm.
- Contractions always. Use "I" and "you." Active voice.
- Be specific: numbers, names, real examples.
- Take a stance. If you've made the point, stop.

## Languages
- Farmer-facing: simple Urdu, "aap" register, short sentences,
  crop and input names as farmers say them (keep Punjabi terms).
- Internal: English, US spelling.
- Never mix scripts in one message unless I ask.

## Formatting
- Numbers as digits. NO em dashes.
- Bold sparingly. Sentence case headers.

## Banned words (English)
delve, leverage, harness, unlock, seamless, robust,
streamline, elevate, supercharge, game-changer, crucial,
pivotal, tapestry, realm, showcase, foster, empower,
cutting-edge, revolutionize, dive in, in today's world

## Banned patterns (fatal)
- "This isn't X, it's Y." / "Not X. Y."
- "It's not about X, it's about Y."
- Any sentence that negates a framing then corrects it.
Fix: delete everything before the positive claim.

## Also avoid
- Rule of three ("fast, clean, and simple")
- "It's worth noting" / "let's dive in" / "moving forward"
- "I'd be happy to" / "great question"
File 3 · my-department.md

What your team is working toward

Keep prompting in the same session you used for about-me. This file is only goals and decisions, no identity overlap.

paste after about-me is done 
You are building my my-department.md file for my Claude folder. This tells Claude what my team at Bakhabar Kissan is working toward right now so it makes better calls on every task. My about-me.md already covers who I am, so no overlap. Goals and decisions only.

Interview me using AskUserQuestion (6-8 questions), one at a time, then compile a condensed my-department.md under 1,000 tokens.

Cover: my department's top 2-3 goals this year with specific numbers, the crop seasons or business cycles that shape my calendar (Rabi, Kharif, month-end, campaign launches), who reads my work and how they like to receive it, the one metric that means success, and what we're actively saying no to.

Output structure:
# MY DEPARTMENT
## Goals   (bullets, numbers where possible)
## Calendar and cycles   (2-4 bullets)
## Who reads my work   (name, role, format they want)
## Saying no to   (bullets)

Under 1,000 tokens. Save as my-department.md in my ABOUT ME/ folder.

Update it when priorities actually change. Once a season is about right. This file is a compass, and not your to-do list.

The connective tissue

CLAUDE.md tells Claude what your files mean

Create a file called CLAUDE.md at the root of your Claude folder. Claude Code reads it at the start of every session there. Paste this in and adjust the file names to yours.

CLAUDE.md · at the root of the Claude folder 
This is my Claude folder. You work inside it.

Before any and every task, read these core files in ABOUT ME/:
- about-me: who I am, what I love and hate (plus an index of my reference files)
- anti-ai-writing-style: write, then audit your draft against this file
- my-department: my team's goals and what we're working toward

ABOUT ME/reference/ holds detailed files for repeat tasks. Read one only
when the task matches its summary in about-me. Don't load the rest.

Never read OUTPUTS/ or TEMPLATES/ unless I point you there. When I do, read that folder's INDEX.md first, then open only what the task needs.

Save all deliverables in OUTPUTS/ in a subfolder named after the project. After saving, add or update the file's row in that folder's INDEX.md (file, created, updated, one line on what it is) and set its Last updated date. Create the index if the folder doesn't have one.

Never invent a number, a name, or a source. If the input doesn't contain it, say so and ask.

If the brief is unclear, use AskUserQuestion. Don't fill gaps with filler. Don't over-explain. Deliver the work.
💡 A question first

How many things can you hold in your head at once?

About 4. Unless you make the things bigger.

"Quarterly tax deadline" is one item to an accountant and 3 to everyone else.

Claude's window is far bigger, and the same rule holds. Fill it with everything and the important lines get skimmed.

Core files lean. Detail in reference/. One line per file in about-me.

inside about-me.md 
## Reference files (read only when relevant)
- monthly-report-sop.md: steps and format for my monthly report
- advisory-format.md: how a farmer advisory is structured, by channel
- data-cleanup-rules.md: how I clean and structure exports
- glossary-urdu.md: crop, input and disease terms and how we write them
- (add more as you need them)

Rule: read a file in reference/ only when the task
matches its summary above. Otherwise skip it.

Build with me · 10 minutes

Set up your folder now

Follow along on your own machine. You'll feel the difference in the build hour if you do this now.

  1. Create a folder named Claude with three subfolders: ABOUT ME, OUTPUTS, TEMPLATES. (Free plan: create a Project on claude.ai instead.)
  2. Open the Claude desktop app, Code tab, open that folder. Pick Opus for this.
  3. Run the about-me interview prompt. Answer out loud.
  4. Keep going into the my-department prompt in the same session.
  5. Drop in your anti-ai-writing-style starter and edit the banned list and language rules.
  6. Save CLAUDE.md at the root of the folder.

I'll run steps 1 to 3 on screen. You start yours. Steps 4 to 6 can finish during the break or the build hour.

The other two rooms

Outputs saves the work. Templates saves the shape.

OUTPUTS/

Where finished work lands

One subfolder per project. Claude files it there and won't read from here on its own, which keeps the token budget for the actual task.

To reuse a past deliverable, just say:

"Read the report in OUTPUTS/monthly-attendance/august."

TEMPLATES/

Your best work, stripped to a skeleton

When Claude builds something you love, end the session with one line:

"Save this as a template in TEMPLATES/."

It keeps the sections, order, and length, drops the content. Next time: "use the template in TEMPLATES/[name]."

Keeping OUTPUTS honest

Every folder gets an INDEX.md

One read instead of a hunt.

Folders run to hundreds of files. Crawling one burns tokens and still misses things. The index is one file at the folder root: every file, one line on what it is and why it exists.

before opening a folder

Read its INDEX.md. Then open only the two files that matter.

after saving anything

Add or update the row. Set the date. Create the index if there isn't one.

if it looks stale

Say so and offer to refresh it. Never work from a wrong map.

Output formats

Work in markdown and HTML. Convert last.

Ask for deliverables as .md or .html while you build. They generate fast, edit instantly, cost fewer tokens, and preview right in the app.

  • Draft and revise in .md or .html. Changes land in seconds.
  • Lock the content first. Only then convert to Word, Excel, PDF, or slides.
  • Data goes straight to .xlsx or .csv. Ask for the file, and for a note on what changed.

Convert once, at the end, when the words are final.

The real bottleneck

You type at 40 words a minute.You talk at 150.

Claude reads 100,000 words in seconds and then waits for you to type a clarifying answer. Across 8 questions that's 10 minutes of you being the slow part.

Use dictation. The desktop app has a microphone button, and tools like Wispr Flow work in any text box, in Urdu and English. Speak your first prompt, your interview answers, and your feedback.

why speaking wins

You give more context when you talk. Typed: "make it simpler." Spoken: "too formal, this is going to a farmer on WhatsApp who reads slowly, keep the dosage numbers exactly, only redo the second paragraph."

Richer input, better output. And it sounds like you.

Don't burn your budget

Five ways to save credits

Claude counts tokens. Every follow-up makes it re-read the whole conversation, so message 30 can cost 30x message 1.

  • Restart, don't argue. When it goes wrong early, edit the earlier message or restart from that point instead of stacking "no, I meant" on top.
  • Fresh session every ~20 messages. Ask for a summary, copy it, start new, paste it as message one. That summary is also your resume note for tomorrow.
  • Batch tasks into one message. "Clean the sheet, summarize the 3 numbers that moved, and draft the email" beats three prompts.
  • Sonnet or Haiku for quick stuff. Grammar, formatting, translation checks. Save Opus for the work that needs it.
  • Keep ABOUT ME small. Bloated files burn tokens before any real work starts.

Two controls worth knowing: Esc stops a run mid-task, and you can type ahead while it works.

💡 Beyond the folder

Connectors, the browser, and scheduled runs

connectors

Reach into your apps

Gmail or Outlook, Google Drive and Sheets, Slack or Teams, your project tool. Claude pulls the data, works on it, and files the result in one session. Start with the one you'd touch daily.

Claude in Chrome

Act inside a web app

A browser extension that lets Claude read a page, fill a form, or pull data from a tool with no connector: the GIS portal, the shop admin, a government form. It asks before it clicks.

scheduled tasks

Run it every Monday

Once a task works by hand, schedule it: the weekly order summary, the Monday content calendar check. This is the Orchestrator seat, and it comes after the task works once.

On "automating approvals and notifications across tools": that layer is connectors plus scheduled runs, or a tool like n8n or Zapier later. Today builds the layer underneath it: a clear task map and a Claude that knows your work. Skip that layer and the automation breaks in a month.

💡 Level up

Turn a repeated workflow into a skill

A skill is packaged instructions for a task Claude should run the same way every time. When you catch yourself re-explaining the same steps, make one.

Bonus: take a long chat where you actually solved a problem, and convert that thread into a skill. The hard thinking is already done, you're saving the recipe.

paste at the end of a chat where you solved something 
Read this whole conversation where we worked out [problem]. Pull the repeatable workflow out of it and build a skill: a SKILL.md with a short description of when to use it, the trigger phrases, and the exact steps in order. Keep it generic so it works next time too, not just for this one case. Save it in .claude/skills/ so I can reuse it.
Skills vs agents

A recipe, or a colleague?

skill

A recipe you run

Packaged steps for a task you repeat. Claude loads it when the task matches. You're still in the session, steering.

Use it when the task repeats and the format is fixed.

advisory-three-formats · monthly-report · visit-report

agent

A colleague you delegate to

Claude runs the whole job without you in the room: on a schedule, on a trigger, or in the background. It comes back with the result.

Use it when the skill has worked by hand 3 times and you can check the result faster than doing it.

Monday order summary · weekly calendar check · survey cleanup

Order matters: prompt, then skill, then agent. An agent is a skill you already trust, running while you do something else.

Do it now · 6 minutes

Map one task from your own week

  1. Pick the task that eats the most hours. Repetitive beats interesting.
  2. List the inputs and where each comes from.
  3. Number the steps. 4 to 7.
  4. Name the output and who uses it.
  5. One sentence: how do you know it's right?
  6. Mark each step H, C, or H+C.
then, one more line

Guess how often each step works.

A percentage next to every step. 95%? 70%? Multiply them and you have how often the whole thing works today.

Worksheet, page 1. Keep it next to your laptop all session.

Before you paste anything

Data rules for the next hour

  • No farmer personal data. Names, phone numbers, CNICs, land records: replace with IDs before it goes in.
  • No salaries, bank details, or unreleased financials unless your manager has cleared the plan you're on for that data.
  • Anonymize in 30 seconds: ask Claude to build you a fake sample with the same columns, or find-and-replace names with F001, F002.
  • When unsure, use the demo seeds in the pack. The system you build works the same on real data next week.
why this matters

Bakhabar Kissan holds data on 15 million farmers. A training exercise is the wrong place to test the data policy. Ask IT which plan you're on and what it permits, then decide what real data goes in next week.

The recipe

Seven steps from task map to saved template

  1. Open Claude Code in your folder (or your Project). Confirm it read CLAUDE.md and ABOUT ME.
  2. Paste your task map as the brief. Inputs, steps, output, who it's for, what "right" means. Speak the extra context.
  3. Attach one real input, anonymized, or a demo seed from the pack.
  4. Ask for the output as .md, .html, or .xlsx, saved in OUTPUTS/[your task]/.
  5. Territory check: open the source next to the output. Spot-check 5 rows or 5 facts. Fix what's wrong.
  6. Red-team it once. Pick one of the four prompts on the next slide.
  7. Save the shape: "Save this as a template in TEMPLATES/." Then write your resume note.

5-minute timer on step 2. If you're still staring at a blank box when it rings, set another and write your next 5 steps instead.

Red-team prompts

Four ways to ask "why is this bad?"

paste after any draft, plan or analysis that matters 
1. Disagreement
"Assume this [draft/plan] is a mistake. Give me the 3 strongest reasons a smart, experienced person in my role would not do it."

2. Base rates
"Out of 100 similar attempts at this, how many got the result I want? Name the reference class you're using."

3. Pre-mortem
"It is 3 months later and this failed. Write the post-mortem: what went wrong, in which week, and why."

4. Fermi
"Break this into pieces. Estimate each piece as a range, multiply them, and tell me the number I should expect."

Saraev's four, lightly adapted. In security this is called red-teaming: attacking your own plan before reality does.

Show and tell · 12 minutes

Three outputs, one red-team, live

  • Volunteer shows the output and the input side by side. What did Claude get right? What did the territory check catch?
  • Room picks one red-team prompt. We run it on screen and read the three objections aloud.
  • Volunteer writes the final sentence: "I'd ship this as is / with these fixes / not yet, because…"

The point of the red-team is to show that a good draft and a finished piece of work are different things, and that the difference is you.
